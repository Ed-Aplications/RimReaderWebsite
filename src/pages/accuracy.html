Accuracy

Is it accurate enough?

For checking and setting toe and camber at home, Rim Reader’s readings repeat to within a few hundredths of a degree from one lap to the next. Here is how that compares with the other ways to check alignment, and what makes the difference.

Which method is right for you

If you only need to check total toe once after a tie-rod change, a tape and a helper may be enough. If you want each wheel’s toe, steer-ahead and thrust without setting up strings, and you will measure more than once, that is what Rim Reader is for. After suspension or steering work, and before highway driving or carrying heavy loads, have a qualified shop check the alignment.

{{/more}} {{more:Where Rim Reader differs|differs}}
  • Against strings and toe plates: no box to square and no tape to read. Like strings, it gives each wheel’s toe against the rear axle, and steer-ahead, which toe plates can’t. It does need a calibrated phone and bracket.
  • Against a plain camber gauge: camber is read against the wheel’s own direction, which makes it {{fact:camber-gain}} more repeatable, and it measures the floor’s slope beside each tire instead of assuming the floor is level.
  • Against other phone gauges: every wheel is read twice with a lift between, each axle’s drift is measured from repeat visits rather than assumed, the bracket and phone are calibrated together, and the thrust angle is read against the car’s body.
  • Against a shop rack: a rack is the reference, measures caster and more, and gives a live readout. Rim Reader is for checking and adjusting at home.

What makes a good reading

  • Two hands, not one. A two-hand hold repeats far better than one hand, and laps close more often.
  • Prongs on the metal lip, never the rubber. Touching the tire roughly doubles the error.
  • A phone that has settled to the outdoor temperature, for {{fact:settle-time}}.
  • The smoothest, flattest floor you can find for camber, wiped clean beside each tire.

Toe, lap after lap

Typical variation from one full lap to the next, on a car that was not moved, touched or steered between laps: rear total toe {{fact:rear-toe-rep}}, front total toe {{fact:front-toe-rep}}, steer-ahead {{fact:steer-rep}}. Each front wheel’s own toe varied 0.037° (front left) and 0.028° (front right), and both axles were within the app’s own pass mark of 0.05°. Front total toe measured four different ways (toe with camber and thrust, toe with thrust, toe only, and the front axle alone) agreed within 0.034°.

Prongs and placement

Letting the prongs touch the tire’s rubber roughly doubled the lift-and-replace error (0.017° against 0.009°). Lifting the bracket off a wheel and setting it back moved the reading by typically about 0.022°, about 0.08 mm across the prongs, which is why the app reads every wheel twice.

Camber

One wheel, session to session: {{fact:camber-rep}} using the top of the rim as a reference, against {{fact:camber-gravity-rep}} from gravity alone, which is how a simple digital angle gauge reads it. Four wheels with the floor correction take about 2½ minutes. Grit and where the bracket sits change the floor readings, so choose the smoothest, flattest floor you can and wipe it.

The phone

Walking round a room and back to the same spot, the phone’s heading came back within a few hundredths of a degree. A phone that is still warming up or cooling down drifts more, so let it settle first.

{{/more}} {{more:About the numbers on screen|display}}

The app shows angles to 0.01°. That is the display, not a claim of 0.01° accuracy: it is finer than the repeatability above. The confidence rating, High, Medium or Low with its reasons, is the better guide to how much a result can be trusted. A Low result is not necessarily wrong, but it is not evidence.
